Normalization — BatchNorm & LayerNorm

BatchNorm or LayerNorm is not a matter of taste — it decides whether your model depends on batch size, and whether it can run inference one sample at a time.

This lesson explains what BatchNorm really does, why Transformers dropped it, and whether to put norm before or after the residual block.

01

Mental model

Every variant is the same normalization; they differ only in the axis used to take the mean and standard deviation.

Initialization only sets the right magnitude at the first step. Normalization is what keeps it right throughout training.

  • After a few hundred updates, each layer's input distribution has drifted from where it was at initialization — each layer has to keep chasing the one before it.
  • Normalization re-pins the mean and variance in the middle of the network, then adds two learned parameters γ and β so the network can regain its freedom if needed.

Setting the right magnitude at the start — the √(1/n) formula, Xavier and He — lives in Weight initialization.
02

BatchNorm

For each feature, compute statistics over the whole batch and force mean 0, variance 1.

x̂=(x − μ)subtract the batch mean/√(σ² + ε)divide by the batch std
y=γ · x̂ + βtwo learned parameters restore freedom
μ, σ²
computed along the batch axis — separately per feature
ε
a tiny number to avoid division by 0
Why are γ and β needed?

Hard normalization to mean 0 variance 1 can cost representational power — for example, sigmoid is nearly linear around 0. These two parameters let the network re-learn a suitable scale and shift, even learning the inverse transform if normalization is harmful.

Two modes — the source of many bugs

During trainingDuring inference
Statistics usedFrom the current batchFrom the running average accumulated during training
Same inputGives different results depending on the other samples in the batchAlways gives the same result
Modemodel.train()model.eval()

Forgetting model.eval()

During evaluation, BatchNorm still uses the current batch's statistics. With small batches the statistics are very noisy, and worse — the prediction for one sample depends on the other samples in the batch. Evaluation results are not reproducible and usually worse than reality. This is the most common silent bug involving BatchNorm.
03

Batch size dependence — practical consequences

batch = 256stable, accurate statistics
batch = 8noisy statistics, clear quality drop
batch = 1variance is 0 — BatchNorm is meaningless

the larger the batch, the more reliable the statisticsBatchNorm borrows information from the other samples in the batcha small batch leaves nothing to borrow

This is not a theoretical problem — it rules out some applications entirely:

SituationProblemSolution
Object detection, image segmentationHigh-resolution images limit the batch to 2–4GroupNorm

This table explains why BatchNorm — despite dominating computer vision for years — barely appears in NLP and generative models.

04

LayerNorm and its variants

The four kinds of norm differ in exactly one thing: which axis the statistics are pooled over.

TypeStatistics overBatch-dependentUsed in
BatchNormBatch axis, per featureYesCNNs with large batches
LayerNormFeature axis, per sampleNoTransformer, RNN
GroupNormChannel groups, per sampleNoDetection, segmentation — small batches
InstanceNormPer channel, per sampleNoImage style transfer
RMSNormLike LayerNorm but without subtracting the meanNoLLaMA and most new LLMs

RMSNorm — why new models switched to it

LayerNorm:  y = γ · (x - μ) / √(σ² + ε)
RMSNorm:    y = γ · x / √(mean(x²) + ε)      ← drops the mean subtraction
Skipping one mean computation and one subtraction makes the norm layer about 10–15% faster with nearly unchanged quality. Experiments show that dividing by the magnitude is the part that matters, while centering contributes almost nothing. This is the kind of detail that shows you follow modern architectures.
05

What BatchNorm really does

The original paper explained it with internal covariate shift: each layer's input distribution changes as earlier layers update, and BatchNorm keeps it stable.

This explanation is repeated everywhere — but later research showed it is probably not the main cause.

Original hypothesisBatchNorm helps because it keeps each layer's input distribution stable (internal covariate shift)
Counter-experiment · Santurkar 2018deliberately inject noise right after the BatchNorm layer to break that stability
Resultthe network still trains as fast and as well as usual → if stabilizing the distribution were the main mechanism, breaking it should lose the benefit; it did not

The explanation considered more accurate: BatchNorm makes the loss surface smoother.

It bounds the magnitude of the gradient and the curvature, so update steps become predictable —

Allowing a larger learning rate without diverging.

Why this detail is worth knowing

Not to refute anyone. But if you state flatly "BatchNorm reduces internal covariate shift" to an interviewer who has read the counter-paper, you lose points. A safe and accurate phrasing: "The original paper explained it with internal covariate shift, but later work suggests the main mechanism is probably smoothing the loss surface, allowing a larger learning rate." This shows you have read both.
06

Where to put the norm

Before or after activation

conv → BN → ReLUoriginal paper · still the most common default
conv → ReLU → BNsometimes slightly better, rarely used
BN → ReLU → convpre-activation · ResNet v2 · more stable for very deep nets

Pre-LN and Post-LN in Transformers

Post-LN — original paper

x = LN(x + attn(x))
LayerNorm sits on the residual path, cutting across it.

Pre-LN — today's models

x = x + attn(LN(x))
LayerNorm sits inside the side branch; the residual path is clean end to end.

Post-LNPre-LN
Residual pathCut by LayerNormCompletely clean end to end
StabilityNeeds a careful learning-rate warmupStable, little warmup needed
Very deep netsHard to trainTrains hundreds of layers
Used inOriginal Transformer, BERTGPT, LLaMA, almost every new model

The deeper reason: with Pre-LN, the gradient has a path from the loss function back to the first layer that passes through no normalization at all —

Exactly the argument for residual connections in the backprop lesson. With Post-LN, every block has a LayerNorm blocking that path.

Implementation detail: drop the bias when a norm follows

With conv → BN the conv bias is redundant: BatchNorm subtracts the mean, so any added constant cancels out, and BN's β already plays the role of the bias. Set bias=False to save parameters and an addition. The same reasoning applies to Linear → LayerNorm.
07

Weight decay and norm

Weight decay = L2 regularization: penalize the sum of squared weights — but not for every parameter.

apply weight decayweight matricesconv, linear · multi-dim tensors
do not applynorm γ and βscale parameters, not representation
do not applybiasonly shifts values
case by caseembeddingusually not
Why doesn't weight decay penalize γ?

It is a scale parameter, not a representation parameter. Pushing γ to 0 means switching off the signal through that layer — not making the model simpler but making it less capable. Likewise, β and the bias only shift values and have nothing to do with the complexity of the function the model represents.

# the right way in PyTorch — split parameters into two groups
decay, no_decay = [], []
for name, p in model.named_parameters():
    if p.ndim <= 1 or name.endswith(".bias"):   # norm params and biases are 1-D tensors
        no_decay.append(p)
    else:
        decay.append(p)

optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW([
    {"params": decay,    "weight_decay": 0.1},
    {"params": no_decay, "weight_decay": 0.0},
])

A note on Adam and AdamW

In the original Adam, weight decay is added to the gradient, so it gets divided by Adam's adaptive scale — the effective strength differs across parameters and is no longer true L2. AdamW decouples the decay from the gradient and applies it directly to the weights, so it is real L2. That is why AdamW is the default for every large model today.
08

Diagnosing with statistics

When a network does not learn, two quantities are worth printing before anything else:

# 1. activation standard deviation per layer
for name, m in model.named_modules():
    m.register_forward_hook(
        lambda mod, inp, out, n=name: print(n, out.std().item()))

# 2. gradient norm per layer
for name, p in model.named_parameters():
    if p.grad is not None:
        print(name, p.grad.norm().item())
ObservationDiagnosisFix
Activation std shrinks to 0 with depthInitialization too small, or missing normHe init, add norm
Activation std grows exponentiallyInitialization too largeLower the init scale, add norm
Gradient norm near 0 in early layers, normal in late layersVanishing gradientsReLU, residual, norm
Gradient norm exactly 0Computation graph is broken — a detach(), no_grad(), or in-place assignmentTrace the propagation path back
Gradient norm spikes abnormallyExploding gradients, often from a batch with outliersGradient clipping
Loss becomes NaN after a few stepsLearning rate too large, or a division by 0 somewhereLower the learning rate; check for log(0) and sqrt of negatives

Reference level

In a well-initialized network, activation standard deviation should be roughly flat across layers — fluctuating by a few tens of percent is normal, shrinking or growing tenfold is not. That is the whole point the lab in section 04 illustrates, and the first test to run on a hand-built architecture.
09

Choosing a norm

SituationChoose
CNN image classification, batch of 32 or moreBatchNorm
Object detection, segmentation, batch 2–8GroupNorm
Transformer, NLP, generative modelsLayerNorm in Pre-LN placement
LLMs where every percent of speed mattersRMSNorm
RNNLayerNorm — BatchNorm does not fit the time axis
Reinforcement learningLayerNorm, or no norm
Serving single requests, no batchAnything that does not depend on the batch

And for initialization, the short rule: He for ReLU, Xavier for tanh and sigmoid, biases at 0.

For very deep ResNets, also set γ = 0 in the last norm layer of each block.

10

Common mistakes

These five mistakes account for almost all misuse of normalization layers.

Forgetting model.eval()

BatchNorm uses batch statistics during evaluation; one sample's prediction depends on the other samples in the batch.

BatchNorm with very small batches

Noisy statistics, quality drops. Use GroupNorm.

Applying weight decay to γ and biases

Pushing the scale to 0 switches the signal off; it does not regularize. Split parameters into two groups.

Keeping a bias in the layer right before a norm

Redundant, because the norm subtracts the mean and already has β.

Flatly claiming BatchNorm reduces internal covariate shift

There is research refuting it. Mention both explanations.
