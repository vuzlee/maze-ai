Weight initialization

Whether a deep network breaks or trains often comes down to nothing more than the multiplier used at initialization.

This lesson derives the √(1/n) formula from scratch and tells Xavier apart from He.

Then it shows why zero weights keep every neuron in a layer identical forever.

01

What weight initialization is

Signal magnitude drifts as it passes through many layers — shrinking toward 0 or blowing up toward infinity. The gradient drifts with it, and the network stops learning.

Initialization

Sets the right magnitude at the very first step — Xavier, He.

Normalization

Forces the magnitude back into range after every layer, throughout training — BatchNorm, LayerNorm, RMSNorm.

Remember one sentence

Initialization is only right at the first step — after a few thousand updates, the weights have moved far from their initial values. Normalization is what keeps signal magnitude stable throughout training. That is why you need both, not one or the other.
02

The root problem — magnitude drift

Each layer multiplies the signal by some factor. Across L layers, that factor gets raised to a power:

factor 0.8 · over 30 layers0.001 — vanishes
factor 1.0 · over 30 layers1.0 — unchanged
factor 1.2 · over 30 layers237 — explodes

A small deviation from 1.0 is enough.Over many layers it grows exponentially.

Vanishing signal

Activations in deep layers are nearly 0

Gradient reaching the early layers is nearly 0 → early layers do not learn

Loss drops very slowly or stalls

Exploding signal

Activations are huge, sigmoid and tanh saturate

Gradient is huge → update steps are too large → loss becomes NaN

Loss oscillates wildly, then diverges

The key point: this is the same problem seen from two sides, both coming from magnitude that is not kept under control.

So the two fixes — proper initialization and normalization — aim at one goal: keep each layer's factor close to 1.

03

Initialization — deriving the formula

z = Σ wi xione neuron with n inputs
Var(z) = n · Var(w) · Var(x)assuming w and x independent, zero mean
Var(z) = Var(x)want magnitude constant across layers ⇒ std(w) = √(1/n)where the square root comes from
Xavier / Glorot — 2 / (n_in + n_out)for tanh, sigmoid: balances the forward pass (needs 1/n_in) and backward pass (needs 1/n_out) by averaging them
He / Kaiming — 2 / n_infor ReLU: ReLU zeroes half its inputs, so output variance is halved; the factor 2 compensates exactly for that
Orthogonal — orthogonal matrixfor RNNs: preserves vector norm across many time steps
Truncated normal — cut at 2 standard deviationsfor Transformers: avoids a few oversized weights from the distribution tails

A concise answer

"The variance of a sum of n independent terms is n times the variance of each term, so to keep signal magnitude constant across layers you need Var(w) = 1/n. He adds a factor of 2 to make up for what ReLU cuts off; Xavier averages n_in and n_out to balance both directions of propagation." Being able to derive it like this is far better than just remembering the formula names.

The standard deviation stays almost flat across all 20 layers — exactly what the formula was designed to achieve.

05

Why not initialize to zero or a constant

If every weight in a layer is equal:

    → every neuron in the layer computes the SAME value
    → every neuron receives the SAME gradient
    → every neuron is updated IDENTICALLY
    → they stay identical forever

The layer has 512 neurons but behaves like ONE single neuron.

This is called the symmetry problem, and randomness in initialization exists precisely to break it.

Not because random is "better" in any sense.

But because it is the only way for neurons in the same layer to learn different things.

Three exceptions where zero initialization is right

Bias. Symmetry is already broken by the weights, so initializing bias to 0 is perfectly fine and is the default.

Last layer of a residual block. Initializing γ = 0 in the block's final BatchNorm makes the block an identity mapping at the start.

The network therefore starts out like a shallow network and gradually deepens.

This trick makes training very deep ResNets more stable.

Forget-gate bias in LSTM. Initialized to 1 instead of 0, so the gate defaults to "remember" and information is not erased from the first step.

06

Common mistakes

Initialization bugs usually give no warning — the network still runs, it just does not learn.

Initializing every weight to zero

The whole layer behaves like a single neuron, forever.
