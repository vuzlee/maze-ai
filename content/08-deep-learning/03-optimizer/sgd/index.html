Deep learning · Optimizer

SGD & mini-batch

Gradient descent on a sample of rows: why the noise is cheap and even helps, batch size, and the learning rate as the first knob.

01

Mental model

Every optimizer reads the gradient at the current weights and turns it into one step; they differ only in that rule.

  • The toy for the whole Optimizer group: eight rows, a model ŷ = w1x1 + w2x2, mean squared error. x2 has a third of the spread of x1, so the loss is a long thin valley — the shape that makes optimizers differ.
  • Where the gradient comes from is Backpropagation; an optimizer only decides what to do with it.
02

Mini-batch

Step against the gradient: which rows it is computed on and how far to step are the two knobs.

wt+1new weights = wtcurrent weights − ηlearning rate ∇LB(wt)gradient on batch B

Compute the gradient on a small random batch instead of all rows: each step is noisier but far cheaper.

  • Full batch = every row per step; SGD strictly = one row; in practice "SGD" means a mini-batch of 32–4096 rows.
  • The noise is not only a cost: it shakes the weights out of sharp, narrow dips, which tends to generalise better.
03

Learning rate

Too small crawls, too large overshoots across the valley further each step and diverges.

  • The narrow direction sets the limit: plain GD diverges once η passes 2 / (largest curvature), even while the long direction still crawls.
  • A loss that jumps to nan in the first few hundred steps is almost always this — lower the rate or add warmup.
04

Learning-rate schedule

Change η over time: warm up from small, then decay — a peak that diverges as a constant now converges.

ηtafter warmup = ηpeaktop rate · ½ (1 + cos(π t − twT − tw))cosine from 1 down to 0
  • Warmup protects the first steps, when the weights are random and Adam's statistics are still empty; then a large rate makes fast progress and the decay lets the weights settle.
  • Linear warmup + cosine decay is the default for transformers; step decay (×0.1 at fixed epochs) is the older CNN recipe.
