ML system
design

The interview round has no correct answer, only a good or poor process.

This lesson gives a seven-step framework that works for any prompt, and explains why almost every large-scale system is two-stage.

Plus how to work out the latency budget and machine count, and the probing questions interviewers always ask.

45-minute interviewthe most-skipped part is the most heavily graded

Remember one sentence

The interviewer assesses how you narrow an open-ended problem, not whether you remember some architecture. Jumping straight to "I'd use a transformer with embeddings" in minute two is the fastest way to fail — it shows you never checked what problem you are solving.
02

Step 1 — clarify requirements

Spend 5–10 minutes asking before you draw anything. This is the most heavily scored part and the most often skipped.

Goalhow is success measured — revenue, engagement, or cost reduction? Who are the users?
Scalehow many users, how many items? Average and peak QPS?
Latencyhow fast must it respond? Can it be precomputed in batch, or must it be real time?
Constraintsbudget, headcount, explainability requirements, legal constraints on data
Scopethe whole system or one part? Which parts can be assumed to exist?

The three questions worth asking if you only get three

1. "Which business metric are we trying to improve?" — it shapes everything else.

2. "Roughly what scale and peak QPS?" — decides one-stage vs two-stage, precomputed vs real time.

3. "What is the latency budget?" — decides how complex the model can be.

03

Step 2 — frame it as an ML problem

The same request "recommend videos the user will like" can be framed four ways, and each fails in a different way.

Classification — click probabilitysimple, labels ready · but optimizing clicks breeds clickbait
Regression — watch timecloser to real value · but biased toward long videos
Ranking — relative orderthe right task · but needs comparison data
Multi-objectiveclicks + watch time + shares + report rate, weighted together — what real systems do

Name several framings, then pick one and say why

This is where points are scored most clearly. It shows you understand that choosing the objective is a product decision, not a technical one. And naming the weakness of your chosen framing — e.g. optimizing clicks leads to clickbait — is exactly what the interviewer is looking for.

And always ask: is ML needed?

Many problems can be solved with rules or simple statistics, far cheaper and easier to operate. "Show the newest videos in the categories the user follows" is a no-ML baseline that is very hard to beat. If you name that baseline and then explain where ML wins, your answer is much stronger than assuming ML by default.
04

Step 3 — two-tier metrics

Offline · AUC, PR-AUC, NDCG, recall@kiterate many times a day, no need to wait for users
Online · click rate, watch time, revenue, retentionthe final yardstick, measured via A/B test
Guardrail · p99, error rate, report rate, churn ratemust not get worse, even if the main metric rises

The sentence to say out loud: an offline metric is only useful if it correlates with the online metric, and that correlation must be verified on historical A/B data —

Never assumed. Plenty of projects improve AUC for months without moving the business metric at all.

The cause is always that this verification step was never done.

Guardrails are the mark of someone who has run real systems

A model that lifts clicks 5% but pushes p99 from 100ms to 400ms is a model that cannot ship. A model that lifts engagement but raises reports of bad content is a brand risk. Listing guardrails up front at the metrics step — not when asked — is a clear plus.
05

Step 4 — data and labels

Where do labels come from?user behavior (cheap, plentiful, biased) or human annotators (expensive, scarce, more accurate)
How late do labels arrive?clicks are immediate; "did the customer churn" takes 30 days → sets retraining frequency
Where do negatives come from?shown but not clicked is negative — but what was never shown is unknown. The core bias
Is there a feedback loop?the model only learns from what the old model showed → it reinforces its own bias
How imbalanced are the classes?0.1% fraud changes everything: metric, threshold, sampling

Feedback loop — always raise it, with the fix

If the model only learns from what it has shown, it never learns whether items it never showed anyone are any good. The standard fix: inject a slice of random traffic — say 1–5% — to collect unbiased data, and log each item's display probability so training can correct for it. Naming both the problem and the fix is one of the biggest pluses in this round.
06

Step 5 — why it is almost always two-stage

Scoring 100 million items with a good model is impossible —

Even at 1 microsecond per item that is already 100 seconds.

So you funnel: cheap first, expensive later.

Why the two stages have different goals

Stage 1 optimizes recall because mistakes here cannot be fixed — an item dropped here is never seen by stage 2. Stage 2 optimizes precision because it only has to choose well from the set it has. Stating this split, and that stage 1 recall is the ceiling of the whole system, shows you understand the architecture rather than just remembering it.

This pattern shows up everywhere: content recommendation, search, ads, and RAG too —

Where vector retrieval is stage 1 and a cross-encoder reranker is stage 2.

Recognizing it as the same pattern is a good connection to point out.

07

Step 5b — features

User · account age, interaction history, inferred interestsmust handle new users
Item · category, age, popularity, content featurespopularity causes a feedback loop — the popular gets pushed more
Interaction · how many items in the same category already viewedusually the strongest feature group
Context · time, device, location, current sessioncheap and often a good signal
Real time · the last few actions in the sessionstrongest signal but hardest on infrastructure

Feature store — why it exists

Features must be computed twice: once on historical data for training, once on live data for serving. If the two pipelines differ — different missing-value handling, different time zones, different rounding — the model drifts without any error, it just gets worse in a puzzling way. That is training–serving skew, and a feature store is the single place that defines features for both pipelines. Raising this in a system design round almost always gets credit.

A related detail: when computing aggregate features for training data, every aggregation must be cut off at the prediction timestamp.

"Customer's total orders" computed over the full history is temporal leakage —

See the Overfitting & regularization lesson.

08

Step 6 — serving

Batch precomputenear-zero latency, cheapest · but loses in-session signal — recommendation emails, home page lists
Real timecan use just-happened behavior · but latency must be managed — search, feed, fraud
Hybridstage 1 precomputed, stage 2 scored per request — what most real systems do

The latency budget must be split into numbers, not a vague "it has to be fast".

network · load balancing20 ms
feature fetch30 ms — surprisingly expensive
candidate generation40 ms
ranking60 ms
re-rank10 ms
headroom40 ms — to absorb peaks and the p99 tail

200 ms allowed in totalwhen the budget runs out you must cut somewhere, there is no other way

Breaking this table down into concrete numbers is one of the quickest ways to impress in a system design round —

It shows you think of the system as a finite budget, not a chain of connected boxes.

09

Lab: system budget

Enter QPS and per-stage latency to see total latency, machines needed, and monthly cost.

Raise the cache hit rate to 50%: machine count and cost halve without touching the model.

That is why the first thing to say when discussing cost is always caching.

Then try raising ranking time to 150ms to watch the budget break.

10

Step 7 — monitoring

Feature distributionshift from training time — the earliest signal, arriving before metrics drop
Prediction distributionmean score drifting, or the over-threshold rate changing abnormally
Performance on true labelsonly available after the label delay — cannot be the only signal
Business metricsthe final yardstick, but noisy from many factors outside the model
System metricsp99 latency, error rate, feature-fetch timeout rate
Missing-value ratea feature source dies silently — very common and very hard to spot

Why watching feature distributions matters most

Because true labels arrive late — maybe 30 days later. If you rely only on label-based performance, you find incidents a month later. Feature distributions are available immediately, and they catch a whole class of infrastructure incidents: a field renamed at the source, an API returning empty values, a dead ETL job. This is the only early-warning system you have.

On retraining: frequency is set by how fast drift happens, not by habit.

Fraud has attackers who adapt constantly, so it may need daily retraining;

Product image classification can go monthly. And you always need a path to roll back to the old version —

The new model may be worse.

11

Issues that always come up

1. Cold start

New userscontext features (device, location, referrer) · ask interests at sign-up · show popular content then adapt within the session
New itemscontent features instead of interaction-learned features · reserve a slice of traffic for exploration
Both newonly content and context features remain — this is why an ID-only model is not enough

2. Extreme class imbalance

0.1% fraud: use PR-AUC instead of accuracy, pick the threshold by expected cost, and resample only on each fold's training set —

Never before the split. See the two lessons on the Machine learning shelf.

3. Explainability requirements

Finance and healthcare often have legal requirements to explain decisions.

Then a linear model or a shallow tree may be the mandatory choice even if less accurate —

Or use a strong model with SHAP, but check whether that level of explanation meets the legal requirement.

12

Worked example — card fraud detection

The same seven steps, run once end to end on a real prompt — the numbers are illustrative.

1 · Clarifyblock fraudulent transactions · 10,000 transactions/sec at peak · decide within 100ms, synchronously in the payment flow · fraud ~0.1% · wrongly blocking real customers is very costly
2 · Framebinary classification returning a probability, not a decision — the threshold is config, three levels: allow / step-up verification / block
3 · Metricoffline PR-AUC (positives too rare for ROC-AUC) · online dollar losses and false-block rate · guardrail p99 under 100ms
4 · Datalabels from cardholder disputes, arriving up to 60 days later · blocked transactions never get a true label → let a very small slice through to collect unbiased data
5 · Modelbaseline is the risk team's rules, kept in parallel as a safety net · main model is gradient boosting on tabular features: amount vs the card's habits, geographic distance, frequency over 1 min / 1 hour / 1 day, unknown device
6 · Servingmust be real time · windowed count features kept in Redis · 15ms features + 20ms model + 65ms headroom · on timeout fall back to rules, never block the payment flow
7 · Monitoringhourly feature and score distributions · block rate per segment · retrain weekly because fraudsters adapt fast

The three most notable details in this example

The model returns a probability; the threshold is config. Changing risk appetite needs no retraining.

Fall back to rules on timeout. The model may be slow or die; the payment flow must not.

Blocked transactions never get a true label. This is the classic feedback loop.

Naming it along with the fix is the strongest point of the whole answer.

13

Common mistakes

Jumping to the model in minute two

Skips the most heavily scored part. Spend 5–10 minutes clarifying requirements.

Not asking about scale

Without QPS and item count you cannot decide between one stage and two.

No baseline

Without a reference point nobody knows how much ML contributes.

Ignoring label source and label delay

They determine retraining frequency and how you monitor.

Not mentioning the feedback loop

The system reinforces its own bias. You need a slice of exploration traffic.

No plan for when the model dies

Every real system needs layered fallbacks.
