Spot it by numbers, not by feel

Overfitting and underfitting are not visible by intuition. They are two numbers side by side: the error on the training set and the error on the validation set.

  • Why those two numbers separate two kinds of failure, and why you cannot fix both at once — see Bias–variance tradeoff.
  • How to split data so these two numbers can be trusted is in Train/val/test & cross-validation.

03

Learning curve — need data or need model

The table above says how the model is broken. The learning curve goes further: is collecting more data worth it? — and the answer does not depend on the data, it depends on the model: same dataset, same 4,980 extra samples bought, one model cuts its error by 97% and the other by 6%.

  • Labeling is the most expensive part of an ML project. This figure answers "are 10,000 more samples worth it" with evidence, before you spend the money.
  • Read the gap, not the height: degree 1 at n = 20 has error 0.755 — twelve times lower than degree 9, yet it is the model that should not get more data.
  • The noise floor is the level no model can go below. Two lines meeting at the floor means the job is done; meeting above the floor is underfitting.
04

How to regularize

Regularization is any way of deliberately constraining the model so it does not memorize noise — at the cost of being slightly more wrong on the training data.

  • It comes in many forms: add a penalty term to the loss, cut the model's capacity, stop early, or add noise to the data.
  • Every form is the same trade: accept a little more bias in exchange for much less variance. They differ in what they pay with.
MethodMechanismUsed for
More dataNoise is harder to memorize with many samplesAlways best if feasible
L2 (weight decay)Penalize the sum of squared weights → weights uniformly smallLinear models, neural networks
L1Penalize the sum of absolute values → some weights become exactly 0When you suspect most features are useless
DropoutRandomly switch off some neurons during training → the network does not rely on a few pathsNeural networks; off at inference
Early stoppingStop when validation error starts to riseAny iterative method — boosting, neural networks
Data augmentationGenerate plausible variants of samples — flip images, swap synonymsImages, audio, text

Reducing complexity is also regularization, even if it is rarely called that: lower a tree's max_depth, use fewer network layers, drop features. Usually the fastest thing to try.

λ = 0no restraint · overfit

  • Order to try: reduce complexity first (cheap, fast), then parameterized regularization, and only last collect more data.
05

Hyperparameter tuning

Regularization strength is a hyperparameter — and finding it is a search problem with a budget. How you spend that budget matters more than people think.

MethodMechanismPros / cons
Grid searchTry every combination on a predefined gridExhaustive, but combinations grow exponentially with the number of parameters
Random searchSample randomly in the spaceUsually better than grid for the same budget — see below
Bayesian / TPEUse past trials to guess which region is worth trying nextMost efficient when each trial is very expensive; adds complexity
Successive halvingTry many configurations with few resources, prune gradually, pour resources into the survivorsVery efficient for neural networks and boosting

Why random beats grid

Same 9 trials, two parameters, but only one really matters — measured on a degree-9 model with 40 samples, λ decides almost everything while the other parameter changes almost nothing:

  • The advantage does not come from luck but from how many distinct values get tried on the axis that matters: a 3×3 grid tries 3, random with 9 runs tries 9.
  • So it holds only when a few hyperparameters dominate — which is almost always the case in practice.
  • For parameters spanning several orders of magnitude — learning rate, regularization strength — sample on a log scale: sampling uniformly between 0.0001 and 0.1 puts 99% of samples above 0.001.
06

Good validation, bad production

Fixing overfitting on the offline set does not finish the job. There is another layer of failure that only shows up when the model meets real data.

CauseSymptomCheck
Data leakageUnusually high offline scoreInspect each feature: is this value available at prediction time?
Wrong splitShuffled time series, or the same group on both sidesRe-split by time or by group and measure again
  • The two causes above are flaws of the offline set itself, so they can be fixed within this lesson.
  • The remaining group cannot: real data changes over time (data drift, concept drift), or features at training and at serving time are computed by two different pieces of code (training–serving skew — the reason feature stores exist).
  • The cost of that group is building monitoring and retraining, i.e. moving from a model problem to a system problem: see Model serving & monitoring and ML system design.

Reading a real case

Each section above teaches one gate. With a real result in hand you go through all four in order — and the order is what none of the sections above can tell you.

  • The four gates do not replace each other: ② says whether to spend money, ③④ say what to do while you cannot spend yet.
  • Both curves in the right panel hit bottom then rise — overcorrect and you fall into underfitting, so λ and complexity must both be chosen with validation, not guessed.
  • The 0.41 minimum is still well above the 0.300 noise floor: regularization cannot replace data, it only buys time until data arrives.
  • Why you cannot fix both bias and variance at once: Bias–variance tradeoff. Shape of the L1 and L2 penalty regions: Ridge, Lasso & ElasticNet.
08

Common mistakes

Fixing overfitting by switching models

Switching to a stronger model while variance is high makes it worse. Read the diagnosis first, pick the fix second.

Adding data when the problem is bias

Once the learning curves have met, more samples just cost money. You need new features or a stronger model.

Increasing regularization until it underfits

Overcorrect and you fall to the other side. Always watch both numbers, not just the gap between them.

Forgetting to turn off dropout at inference

Results fluctuate between runs and fall short of what the model can really do.

Boosting without early stopping

With a large n_estimators overfitting is certain, only a matter of when.

Sampling learning rate on a linear scale

You miss the region of small values. Use a log scale.
