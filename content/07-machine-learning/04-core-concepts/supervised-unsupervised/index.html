Supervised, unsupervised & reinforcement

The first step of every design problem: name the problem correctly. Not to classify for neatness, but because the problem type decides what you measure with — and measure wrong, and everything after is meaningless.

01

Mental model

The lesson is the four ideas below. Each later section redraws this map and highlights the cell being studied.

02

Classifying the problem

The three kinds of learning are split by the feedback signal the machine receives — not by algorithm, not by data type.

The shelf opener already told what the three kinds are.

Here is the more practical part: facing a real problem, how do you tell which kind it is?

And why getting this wrong ruins the whole project.

Supervised

every sample has the right answer

Unsupervised

nothing to score against

Reinforcement

you only know good or bad after acting

  • The real difference is whether there is an objective: supervised has a per-sample loss, reinforcement only a delayed reward, unsupervised none.
  • Many problems look unsupervised but are really supervised problems not yet named correctly.
  • Labels give you a ruler, so you should almost always try to turn the problem into a supervised one.
03

Where labels come from

"Are there labels" has no ready answer — labels are made, and their cost decides whether the project is feasible.

Already in the business processcancelled orders, defaulted loans, clicks · almost free, but prone to data leakage
Labeled by handmedical images, document categories · expensive, slow, inconsistent across labelers
Inferred from behaviorwatched the whole video → counts as liked · cheap, but only an approximate label
Generated from the data itselfmask a word and make the model guess it · free and unlimited — the basis of self-supervised, section 04
  • The hardest part isn't choosing the algorithm but consistent labels: if two labelers agree 70% of the time, that's your performance ceiling.
  • Labels inferred from behavior carry the bias of the system already running: users can only click what was shown.
  • Labels already in the business process are where data leakage happens most — see Train/val/test & cross-validation.
04

Regression hay classification

Split by the type of label, and what changes with it is the loss function and the metric — not the model.

Regression

Label a continuous number — price, duration, temperature

Loss MSE or MAE

Measured by RMSE, MAE, R²

Question answered "how much"

Classification

Label one of a few classes — spam/not spam, three risk levels

Loss cross-entropy (log loss)

Measured by precision, recall, AUC

Question answered "which kind"

  • The same linear model, with a different output wrapper and a different loss, moves from one side to the other — Linear regression and Logistic regression share one skeleton.
  • Why MSE goes with regression and log loss with classification is not a convention: both fall out of maximum likelihood under two different distributional assumptions.
  • A numeric label does not automatically mean regression: 1–5 stars are numbers, but the gap 1→2 isn't the same as 4→5.
  • Many problems should return a probability, not a label — the threshold is a business decision (Metric & confusion matrix).
  • Multi-class and multi-label are two different things: belonging to exactly one class, or carrying several labels at once. Pick wrong and you mix up softmax and sigmoid.
05

When labels are too expensive

Reality is usually: tons of raw data, very few labels. The four workarounds below all start from one idea — squeeze a learning signal out of the unlabeled data.

Self-supervised — mask part of the data and make the model guess it backuse when you have a mountain of raw data
Semi-supervised — pseudo-label the unlabeled part, then keep traininguse when you have a few thousand labels but millions of samples
Transfer learning — fine-tune a model already trained on a large setthe default for images and text; try this first
Active learning — the model picks the samples it is least sure of to ask aboutuse when the labeling budget is limited

Self-supervised is what stands behind every language model today.

  • The label "what is the next word" is already in every sentence ever written.
  • So training data is nearly unlimited and costs nothing to label.
  • That is the technical reason large-scale pretraining became feasible.
  • The cost of semi-supervised: wrong pseudo-labels get reinforced by the model itself, and early errors are amplified round after round.
  • The cost of active learning: the collected set is deliberately skewed toward hard samples, so don't use it to estimate real performance.
  • Try transfer learning before any of the above — it's the cheapest and usually good enough.
