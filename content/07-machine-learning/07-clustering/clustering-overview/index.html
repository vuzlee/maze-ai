Clustering overview

Choosing a clustering algorithm is choosing an assumption about cluster shape. This lesson covers the four assumptions on offer, and how to tell you chose wrong.

01

Mental model

This problem differs from every supervised problem in one fundamental way: nobody tells you what a correct split looks like.

  • Classification has an answer key, so it has accuracy. Clustering does not — all three panels on the right are valid results, different because the three algorithms define "cluster" differently.
  • Only DBSCAN is allowed to say "this point belongs to no cluster"; k-means must put every point somewhere, even a point stranded in the empty space between.
  • A clustering result always exists, even on random data — the algorithm never says "there are no clusters".
  • So the first question is not "which algorithm" but "what do the clusters here look like" — answer that and choosing a family is easy. Many problems sound like clustering but are really classification: "grouping customers" to find out who is about to churn.
02

The four families at a glance

Read the Assumption column first. Whichever assumption fits your data, read on along that row.

  • The first three families split hard — one label per point. Gaussian Mixture splits soft: one probability vector per point.
  • Agglomerative repeatedly merges the two closest clusters, with "close" measured by linkage: ward gives balanced clusters, single link catches long chains.
  • Three of the four families rely on distance, so standardizing the scales is mandatory, and with many dimensions you should reduce them first.
03

Scoring without an answer key

This is the truly hard part of clustering — not running the algorithm, but knowing whether the result can be trusted.

Internal measureslook only at the data and the cluster labels: silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz. They tell whether clusters are tight and separate — but favor round clusters, so do not use them to compare two families with different assumptions
External measureswhen you happen to have true labels to compare against: Adjusted Rand Index, Normalized Mutual Information. Rarely available — and if they are, you should usually just go supervised
Check against outside variablesdo the clusters differ on things the algorithm never saw: churn rate, next month's revenue, complaint rate
Measure by using itcluster, then do something — send three different campaigns — and measure the real outcome. The final measure, and the only one that answers "is this split useful"
  • The figure above is why the bottom two layers matter more than the top two: a high silhouette with clusters that look the same on every business variable is just pretty geometry.
  • It is also why you should not use internal measures to compare two families with different assumptions — they reward round clusters, i.e. they reward k-means before even looking at the data.
  • The cheapest check is the right-hand column: rerun on data with each column shuffled. If the score does not get worse, the structure is made up.
  • Cluster labels are arbitrary numbers, not comparable across two runs — to match them, align by cluster centroid.
04

What order to learn in

Three lessons, following exactly the chain of cause and effect: each was born to fix where the previous one breaks.

1 · K-means & clusteringIterate two steps around k centroids, pick k with the elbow and silhouette, and the four hidden assumptions that make it split wrongly. Read it first because everything after is compared against it.
2 · DBSCANDrops the notion of a centroid and defines clusters by density. Gets arbitrary cluster shapes and a notion of noise, paid for with an eps parameter that is hard to choose.
3 · HDBSCANRuns DBSCAN at every eps level at once and keeps the most stable clusters. Fixes DBSCAN's dead spot: clusters of different density.
  • Agglomerative and Gaussian Mixture have no lessons of their own because they are rare in real work — section 02 is enough to recognize the names when needed.
  • For high-dimensional data, read PCA first: every algorithm here relies on distance, and in high dimensions distance loses its meaning.
