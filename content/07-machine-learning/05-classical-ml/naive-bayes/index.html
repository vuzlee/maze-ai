Naive Bayes

Count words in emails that already have labels, then score a new email. Three things to remember:

  • training is just counting, there is no loop
  • assume words are independent of each other once the class is known
  • that assumption is almost always wrong, yet the label is still usable
01

Mental model

Naive Bayes asks which class most often produces these words. Bayes' theorem flips that question into something countable.

  • The whole lesson runs on exactly 10 emails and a 4-word vocabulary: win, prize, meet, plan.
  • The question for the whole lesson: is the email "plan meet prize" spam or ham?
  • Same family as Logistic regression. That one runs gradient descent; this one is optimal once counting is done.
02

General formula

Bayes turns P(class | words) into P(words | class) × P(class). The right side can be counted from data; the left side cannot.

P(c | x)what we want = P(x | c) · P(c)P(x)numerator countable · denominator same for every class

The denominator is the same for every class, so it can be dropped when comparing. Assuming words are independent lets us split P(x | c) into a product over words, and taking the log turns the product into a sum:

  • Return the class with the highest score. Only the ordering between classes has to be right, not the values.
  • There are no parameters to learn, so no gradient and no early stopping.
  • The word naive refers to the independence assumption: seeing one word says nothing about another. The price is in section 05.
03

From 10 emails to counts

Training is just counting. One pass over the 10 emails and you are done; those 8 numbers are the whole model.

  • The independence assumption is what lets us count each word separately.
  • Counting word pairs would already mean 16 cells for 4 words, with only 10 emails to count from.
  • The assumption is clearly wrong: look at the 6 spam emails and win and prize obviously travel together.
  • A new email is just added to the counts, no retraining from scratch.
  • Ham's prize cell is 0. The next section fixes exactly that.
04

Laplace and log

Two tricks fix two different problems: a zero cell wipes out the evidence, and multiplying many probabilities underflows.

P(word | class) = count(word, class) + αcount(class) + α · Vadding α leaves no cell at 0
α
added to every word, including unseen ones
V
the vocabulary size, here 4
  • Adding α to every count is like having seen each word α times before looking at the 10 emails.
  • Both extremes break, for two different reasons.
  • α = 0 leaves a cell at 0. α = 50 erases all the differences between cells.
  • α = 1 is Laplace, sklearn's default. With a large vocabulary a smaller α usually works better. Pick it with cross-validation.
  • Log is an increasing function, so the class with the larger product also has the larger sum of logs. Changing the scale does not change the winner.
05

A wrong assumption that still works

The independence assumption counts the same evidence several times. The label is still right, but the probability cannot be trusted.

  • The bias shifts every class's score in the same direction, so the ordering between classes usually stays the same.
  • The ranking can be trusted. Using 0.99 as a threshold cannot.
  • If you need trustworthy numbers, calibrate, or switch to Logistic regression.
  • This is exactly what happens when features are correlated, for example win and prize.
06

Pros and cons

Trades accurate probabilities for speed and simplicity. Every pro and con comes from the independence assumption.

AspectGainLoss
TrainingOne counting pass, supports incremental updatesCannot learn interactions between features
Little dataA few hundred samples already work wellUnseen words need α, otherwise the score goes to infinity
DimensionalityTens of thousands of words are no problemCorrelated features get counted several times
ProbabilitiesA score for every class out of the boxOverconfident, cannot be used as a threshold
Reading the modelRead the count table directly, see which word leans toward which classNo learned weights to compare

The three variants differ only in how they turn an email into numbers:

VariantReads email asUse when
Multinomialhow many times each word appearstext, the default
Bernoulliwhether a word appears, ignoring countsvery short emails
Gaussiancontinuous measurements, one bell curve per classnot text
  • Choose Naive Bayes when you need a text baseline that runs in seconds, or data is very scarce.
  • Choose logistic regression when you need trustworthy probabilities or features are highly correlated.
  • Common mistakes: leaving α at its default and stopping there, and trusting the probability without calibrating.
