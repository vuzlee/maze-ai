Naive Bayes

Count words in emails that already have labels, then score a new email. Three things to remember:

  • training is just counting, there is no loop
  • assume words are independent of each other once the class is known
  • that assumption is almost always wrong, yet the label is still usable
01

Mental model

Naive Bayes asks which class most often writes these words, and answers by counting.

  • The whole lesson runs on 10 labelled emails (6 spam, 4 ham) and four words — win, prize, meet, plan — and asks one question: is "plan meet prize" spam or ham?
  • Same job as Logistic regression, which learns weights by gradient descent; Naive Bayes is the text baseline that trains in seconds and works with a few hundred samples.
02

General formula

Bayes flips P(class | words) into things you can count; assuming words are independent turns it into a sum of logs.

2.1Bayes rule and the score

Drop the shared denominator, split per word, take logs: one sum per class.

P(c | x)posterior: what we want = P(x | c) · P(c)P(x)likelihood × prior, over evidence
score(c)compare across classes = log P(c)prior: share of emails in c + Σi log P(ti | c)one term per word of the email
ŷpredicted class = arg maxc score(c)highest score wins
  • P(x) is the same for every class, so it never changes the winner — only the ordering of scores matters.
  • No loss and no gradient: the counts already are the maximum-likelihood estimate.

2.2Independence assumption

Naive = once the class is known, one word says nothing about another.

  • It is what makes counting possible: word pairs would already need 16 cells for 4 words, with only 10 emails to fill them.
  • Its price: words that travel together (win, prize) are the same evidence counted twice, and the model cannot learn interactions — logistic regression splits the weight instead.
03

Train and predict

Train = count words per class. Predict = add up the logs of those counts.

3.1Count

One pass over the 10 emails fills a 2 × 4 table: that table is the model.

  • A new labelled email just adds to the counts — no retraining; read the table directly to see which word leans toward which class.
  • Ham never used prize: that 0 is the problem of 4.1.

3.2Score a new email

Fill one log per word, add the prior, the larger sum wins.

  • Fractions use α = 1 (section 4.1): ham’s 1, 0, 4, 4 of 9 words become 2, 1, 5, 5 of 13.
  • Scores are negative because logs of probabilities are; closer to 0 is better.

3.3Probabilities

Normalising the scores gives a probability — the label is usable, the number is overconfident.

P(c | x)the probability it reports = exp score(c)Σc′ exp score(c′)scores normalised over the classes
  • Repeated or correlated evidence pushes every class’s score the same way, so the ranking survives while the probability races toward 1.
  • Trust the ranking, not 0.99: calibrate before using it as a threshold, or switch to logistic regression.
04

Laplace and log

Two tricks fix two different problems: a zero cell wipes out the evidence, and multiplying many probabilities underflows.

4.1Laplace smoothing α

Add α to every count so no cell is 0.

P(t | c) = count(t, c) + αcount(c) + αVα added to every cell · V = vocabulary size
  • Adding α is like having seen every word α times before reading the data; both extremes break — 0 leaves a zero cell, 50 erases the counts.
  • α = 1 is Laplace (sklearn default); with a large vocabulary a smaller α usually works better — tune it with cross-validation, do not just keep the default.

4.2Log space

A product of many small numbers underflows to 0; a sum of logs never does.

  • log is increasing, so the class with the larger product also has the larger log sum: the winner is unchanged.
05

Variants

The three variants differ only in how a sample becomes numbers.

5.1Multinomial

Reads how many times each word appears.

  • The default for text, and the one used throughout this lesson; tens of thousands of words are no problem.

5.2Bernoulli

Reads present or not, and scores missing words too.

  • Suits very short texts, where a word’s absence carries signal.

5.3Gaussian

For continuous features, each class gets a bell curve per feature.

P(x | c) = 1σc√(2π) exp(−(x − μc)22σ2c)mean μ and spread σ counted per class
  • Training = one mean and one standard deviation per class per feature.
